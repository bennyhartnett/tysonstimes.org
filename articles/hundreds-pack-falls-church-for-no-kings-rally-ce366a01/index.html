Hundreds Pack Falls Church for No Kings Rally

An estimated 800 to 1,000 people lined West Broad Street in downtown Falls Church on June 14 for a muggy morning of protest, patriotic signs and sustained horn-honking from passing drivers.

By Tysons Times Staff

Coverage area
Falls Church
Section
Local
Published
June 16, 2025
Topics
local, falls church, schools, environment

Article Text

An estimated 800 to 1,000 people lined West Broad Street in downtown Falls Church on June 14 for a muggy morning of protest, patriotic signs and sustained horn-honking from passing drivers.

No official attendance figure was available, but the crowd at the “No Kings Day Rally” included residents of different ages, veterans, students, elected officials and first-time activists. Democracy Falls Church organized the nonviolent demonstration as part of a nationwide movement opposing what participants described as authoritarian actions by President Donald Trump and his administration.

The rally coincided with a military parade in Washington marking the U.S. Army’s 250th anniversary and Trump’s 79th birthday. Democracy Falls Church said it supported the Army celebration while presenting its event as a counterpoint to the president’s parade.

The local organization formed in February, bringing together longtime civic leaders, newer residents and novice activists concerned about the administration’s conduct since the Jan. 20 inauguration. The group compared its grievances to those listed against King George III in the Declaration of Independence and argued that Americans must again reject the rule of a would-be king.

Virginia Del. Marcus Simon, who represents Falls Church in the House of Delegates, said the flags and patriotic colors throughout the crowd challenged claims that demonstrators hated the country.

“Everybody out here is out here because they love America,” Simon said, calling protest and the expression of grievances fundamentally American exercises of First Amendment rights. He said he could not have been prouder of Falls Church.

Former City Council member Marty Meserve, wearing a yellow safety vest as an event organizer, said Democracy Falls Church wanted to give residents a safe place to gather after months of calling and writing elected officials.

Meserve said she was thrilled by both the turnout and the response from motorists. She hoped the rally would encourage participants to become more involved, adding that anyone who believes in the Constitution, democracy and the rule of law was welcome.

Navy veteran Blair Stewart said he attended to support the movement and demonstrate that protesters could exercise their rights peacefully without being criminals or seeking trouble.

Stewart, who served from 1976 to 1980, criticized Trump’s use of executive orders and the deployment of federal troops in Los Angeles. He also expressed alarm about proposed cuts affecting veterans and urged Congress to restrain presidential actions it considers unlawful.

Two rising college juniors from Washington, Ruby Lillie and Kate Satterfield, found the Falls Church event through the No Kings website. They declined to identify their Washington-area college but said they chose the local rally because they did not believe it was safe to demonstrate in Washington.

Lillie said she regularly attends pro-democracy protests and was troubled by discussion of Trump seeking a third term and White House imagery depicting him on a throne. Satterfield focused on immigration and human rights, saying families in her home state of Florida were frightened and that people had become afraid to speak publicly.

Army veteran Christine Kirby attended wearing a Gulf War veteran’s cap and a Consumer Financial Protection Bureau button. Kirby said she served for 20 years and now works for the CFPB, where employees had been prevented from carrying out their normal work since February.

She said workers were leaving amid uncertainty about the agency’s future and asserted that the administration wanted to eliminate the bureau. As a former Army judge advocate, Kirby also objected to using military personnel against Americans, removing senior military lawyers and reducing veterans’ benefits.

Another participant, identified only as Rupa, brought her family to demonstrate support for democracy and voice concern about immigration policies separating families. As the child of immigrants, she said those issues struck close to home even when they did not affect her directly.

Rupa wanted her children to witness civic participation early in life so they could help create positive change as they grew older. She described the gathering as a powerful display of solidarity and community.

Some demonstrators declined to provide names, pose for photographs or share much personal information. One man who insisted on anonymity warned that the United States was not immune to dictatorship.

Citing scholarship about democratic decline in countries including Turkey and Hungary, he said Americans needed to resist the erosion of democratic norms immediately. He viewed the central threat not as literal monarchy but as weakened separation of powers, particularly when the executive branch disregards judicial rulings.

Volunteer marshal Kathy Dehm, who distributed bottled water, said she had joined Democracy Falls Church only four weeks earlier and was impressed by how quickly organizers combined their skills to stage the event.

A retired federal employee and former Peace Corps volunteer in Poland from 1997 to 1999, Dehm said her greatest fear was seeing the U.S. military deployed against Americans. Conversations with Polish residents who had lived under communism, along with her visit to Auschwitz, shaped how she interpreted current events.

Dehm also hoped the rally would receive broad media attention. She recalled working on federal budget matters related to the Afghanistan and Iraq wars without realizing how many antiwar demonstrations were occurring at the time.

Newlyweds Darius Reynolds and Ashley Yi, residents of the Tinner Hill apartments, said immigration enforcement, federal job cuts and U.S. support for Israel amid Palestinian civilian deaths helped bring them to the rally.

Yi said her immigrant parents had worked with the federal government before losing their positions through cuts associated with the Department of Government Efficiency. She also condemned the treatment of immigrants and the separation of children from their parents.

Reynolds said a recent visit with his grandmother in San Diego had given him a personal connection to the civil rights era. His grandmother attended school in Selma, Alabama, met Martin Luther King Jr. at her high school and trained to march before participating in a demonstration with him.

After returning home and seeing another protest while attending a wedding in Maine, Reynolds said he recognized a national pattern of people objecting to federal policies they believed were breaking laws and damaging families and communities. He argued that the country was not designed to place unchecked authority in one person.

The couple said they felt welcomed and secure in Tinner Hill, whose deep local history they proudly share with visitors. For Yi, turning onto a street filled with demonstrators offered a reassuring sign of community support during an unsettling period.
