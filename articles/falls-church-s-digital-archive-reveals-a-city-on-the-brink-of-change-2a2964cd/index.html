Falls Church’s Digital Archive Reveals a City on the Brink of Change

Newly digitized records from the Mary Riley Styles Public Library offer a vivid look at Falls Church before it became an independent city—and show how many local concerns have endured...

By Tysons Times Staff

Coverage area
Falls Church
Section
Business
Published
November 11, 2025
Topics
business, falls church, development, public safety

Article Text

Newly digitized records from the Mary Riley Styles Public Library offer a vivid look at Falls Church before it became an independent city—and show how many local concerns have endured for generations.

The Digital Archives of Falls Church History includes photographs, obituaries, Civil War letters, oral histories, City Council minutes, budgets, newsletters and other historical documents. Among its holdings are January 1948 editions of The Fairfax Standard, a weekly newspaper that covered Falls Church when it was still a town within Fairfax County.

Issues from Jan. 2 and Jan. 9 capture a community wrestling with unfinished roads, speeding drivers, rapid development, strained public services and racial segregation. They also document postwar prices, new technology and everyday life.

A Mile of Dust and Mud

One of the newspaper’s sharpest complaints involved the long-delayed paving of Leesburg Pike through Falls Church.

A Jan. 2 editorial described slightly more than a mile of roadwork that had dragged on for more than a year. Motorists had spent months traveling slowly through dust and mud while a construction sign promising future convenience and safety became weather-beaten.

The editorial pressed the state Highway Department to finish the project, noting that asphalt paving was continuing elsewhere in the Washington region.

Transportation was not the only public concern. Falls Church residents were also demanding action against speeding in neighborhoods where children played.

At a Town Council meeting, Walter L. Mess of Seaton Lane presented a safety platform from the Southwest Falls Church Citizens Association. Mayor Jenner Hazelgrove, the council and Town Manager Roy Dunn supported a campaign encouraging residents to report the license numbers of speeding vehicles.

Dunn said reported motorists would receive written warnings. Later offenses, if measured with a stopwatch or speedometer, could lead to fines. The campaign followed injuries to six children, and Mess said residents had exhausted their patience.

By Jan. 9, Falls Church police had reported 10 traffic cases involving allegations such as speeding and reckless driving.

Winter created another transportation challenge. Portions of Poplar Drive, Buxton Road and Highland Avenue were closed for sledding, giving children places to play away from moving vehicles.

The American Automobile Association supported providing outdoor recreation areas for school-age children. It warned that traffic was growing while the average automobile on the road was nine years old, making quick stops more difficult.

Polio, Prices and Postwar Life

Polio remained a major national worry in early 1948. The Jan. 9 newspaper reported figures from the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis showing that about 80,000 Americans had contracted the disease from January 1943 through December 1947.

That represented a 150% increase over the preceding five-year period. The foundation also said the number of children and young people disabled by polio had risen by almost 45% over seven years.

The issues also provide a snapshot of postwar prices. The newspaper cost 7 cents per copy and reported that its sales had doubled since January 1947. One classified advertisement offered a 1935 four-door Chevrolet with a heater and a passed Virginia inspection for $250. Another advertisement promoted a chicken dinner for 35 cents.

The period’s changing technology appeared in a report that the Chesapeake and Potomac Telephone Company had installed dial telephone service in Falls Church late in 1947. Elsewhere in Fairfax County, residents continued to object to extra telephone charges for calls connecting the county with Washington and Alexandria.

Information and political discussion often traveled through neighborhood gathering places. Complaints about inadequate east-west bus service provided by the Washington, Virginia and Maryland Coach Company were reportedly debated in corner grocery stores as well as by organized civic groups.

A Housing Boom Strains Fairfax County

Falls Church was entering a major period of suburban growth as veterans returned from World War II and federal programs expanded access to education and home financing, though those opportunities were not distributed equally across racial lines.

The Jan. 9 edition announced that the first units at Tyler Gardens were expected to open that April. The 481-unit apartment development, now associated with the Winter Hill neighborhood, was under construction along West Broad Street just west of the traffic light. Plans called for masonry buildings containing one-, two- and three-bedroom apartments.

The boom was already placing pressure on schools, roads, sanitation systems and utilities. Approximately 4,000 building permits had been issued across Fairfax County during 1947, indicating an increase of more than 15,000 residents and bringing the county’s population close to 100,000.

Large developments—including Tyler Gardens, King’s Kitchen, John N. Campbell and Westcott—were expected to add to those demands.

Hunting, Greenhouses and Unusual Headlines

The newspaper’s pages also reveal interests that are less prominent in Falls Church today.

An outdoors column reviewed the close of Virginia’s bear and deer seasons and described deer hunting in Caroline County, where access to posted land often required membership in a hunting group or an invitation from one.

Hunters used packs of dogs, sometimes numbering five to 10 or more, to pursue deer toward watchers stationed around the hunting area. One account described hounds becoming exhausted after chasing a fox for three hours before encountering a large buck.

A front-page feature profiled Gilbert Sherfey, manager of the Safeway at Lee Highway and Hillwood Avenue, and the greenhouse he had built behind his Annandale Road home. The 14-by-20-foot structure allowed him to grow poinsettias for Christmas, lilies for Easter and other plants throughout the year.

Sherfey initially heated the greenhouse with a stove but later connected it to his home’s heating system. A separate thermostat helped him maintain temperatures for different plants. Although gardening was primarily a hobby, he also sold plants decorated with bows and foil.

Some headlines carried meanings that are easily misunderstood decades later. “Postmaster Improves” referred not to better mail service but to Falls Church Postmaster B. Frank May’s recovery from severe bronchitis. He had improved enough to spend several hours a day at his desk.

Another brief concerned David Scull of Annandale, who was traveling to China for a State Department administrative survey. He also expected to visit the Philippines and Siam before returning March 20.

Women’s Work Appears Through a Narrow Lens

The 1948 editions document women organizing charitable drives and community activities, although coverage often confined them to subjects then labeled as women’s interests.

Before Christmas, the Fairfax County chapter of the American Red Cross and local women’s clubs assembled decorations, food and gifts for Fort Belvoir Hospital and other military and veterans hospitals in the region.

The Vienna Garden Club also held a holiday doorway-decorating contest. Mrs. Corwin Vasant won a flowering plant as first prize, while Mrs. N. Hugh McDiarmed received honorable mention.

Such coverage showed women contributing to civic life while rarely identifying them beyond socially prescribed roles.

Black Civic Achievement Amid Segregation

The archive also preserves direct evidence of segregation—and of Black residents organizing against it.

A regular feature titled “Colored Citizen’s News” was written by Ollie W. Tinner, a co-founder of the Colored Citizens Association of Fairfax County. Established in Merrifield in 1928, the organization encouraged Black residents to vote and sought better schools, fair treatment in courts and public transportation, broader civic participation and improved relations between racial groups.

The association said it helped secure Black participation in Fairfax County primary elections beginning Aug. 5, 1930. It held a Citizens’ Day Rally at the segregated fairground, with proceeds supporting libraries in Black schools, and successfully pushed two local service stations to remove “White Only” restroom signs.

Tinner was also among the first Black jurors drawn from the association to serve in Fairfax County, on May 24, 1935. As the group’s longtime publicity manager, he helped establish a relationship with the local white press that allowed the organization to publicize its meetings, accomplishments and concerns without censorship.

His Jan. 2, 1948, column celebrated Falls Church Boy Scout Troop 523 members who received ranks, merit badges and recognition from Camp Banneker. It also reported on a $5,000 grant supporting radioactivity research by Howard University physicists, the appointment of George P. Clarke as a regular Black news commentator on a Richmond radio network, and the formation of a biracial advisory council involving Norfolk teachers.

Another item noted that the United Negro College Fund had raised more than $1 million in 57 communities to assist 33 private Black colleges and expand educational opportunities.

Together, the archived pages show Falls Church and Fairfax County at a turning point. Roads and utilities struggled to keep pace with growth, residents demanded safer streets, new homes transformed the landscape and Black civic leaders fought for political participation and equal treatment—all in a community whose modern identity was just beginning to take shape.
