Fairfax Countywide Trash Takeover Plan Stalls After Fierce Pushback

By Tysons Times Staff

Coverage area
Fairfax
Section
Civic Desk
Published
May 13, 2026
Topics
civic, fairfax, environment, transportation

Article Text

Fairfax County leaders have largely backed away from a controversial proposal to create a countywide residential trash district after hearing strong opposition from residents and private haulers.

“We’ve heard pretty loud and pretty clearly that this is not the model,” Providence District Supervisor Dalia Palchik said while chairing the Board of Supervisors’ Environment Committee meeting on May 12.

Supervisors took no formal action to terminate the proposal, but the committee showed little interest in advancing it. Department of Public Works and Environmental Services Director Christopher Herrington said staff would stop working on the plan “unless we hear differently.”

Board Chair Jeff McKay was prepared to let the proposal die, although he said the idea never received enough consideration from the broader public.

“It’s a missed opportunity,” McKay said. “I don’t think it got the fair airing with our everyday citizens.”

About 10% of Fairfax County households currently receive trash service through the county government, primarily in older neighborhoods in the eastern part of the county. Most residents instead contract with private haulers, either individually or through homeowners’ associations.

The public works department formally proposed a possible countywide expansion in late 2024 as part of an update to Fairfax’s solid waste management plan. Under the proposed unified sanitation district, the county would contract with private companies to provide trash and recycling collection rather than leaving those arrangements to property owners and neighborhood associations.

Virginia law allows localities to establish such systems. Arlington has long controlled trash collection in its single-family neighborhoods, and Falls Church operates under a similar model.

County staff argued that centralized contracting could make service more reliable, limit greenhouse gas emissions by reducing the number of collection trucks entering each neighborhood and give Fairfax greater control over prices.

The county would have to provide private collectors with five years’ notice before displacing them. Haulers organized against the proposal after it began gaining attention roughly 18 months ago, and their objections found support among residents.

McKay said county leaders heard those concerns “loud and clear.”

Mount Vernon District Supervisor Dan Storck was the only committee member who supported beginning the required five-year notice period. He argued that starting the clock would give Fairfax more time to examine the idea without committing the county to adopting it.

“I don’t understand why we don’t pull the trigger,” Storck said. “I want to start that five-year clock. Let’s start the clock, let’s start the process.”

Springfield District Supervisor Pat Herrity, who opposed the unified district from the beginning, said he was “happy that we listened” to residents and trash companies.

Herrity and Hunter Mill District Supervisor Walter Alcorn suggested more limited alternatives. Those could include extending county trash service into neighborhoods that want it or finding other ways to reduce the number of haulers sending trucks through the same communities.

Alcorn requested in summer 2025 that the countywide proposal be paused for additional discussion and public input. At the time, he described a unified district as only one possible response to complaints about multiple companies serving individual neighborhoods.

“We have kind of a Wild West out there,” Alcorn wrote in a social media post that summer. “We’re looking at all the options.”

Although supervisors took neither a formal vote nor an informal tally at the May 12 meeting, the message to county staff was clear: the countywide plan is effectively shelved for now.
