Fairfax Road Deaths Fuel High-Stakes Push for Speed Cameras

Article Text

After at least 15 pedestrians were killed and roughly 100 were seriously injured in Fairfax County traffic crashes in 2025, Northern Virginia safety advocates are pressing state lawmakers to fund road improvements and expand automated speed enforcement.

The campaign follows an especially deadly December, when five pedestrians died within 22 days. Northern Virginia Families for Safe Streets says the toll highlights the consequences of vehicle speed and dangerous road design.

“You might survive being hit at 20 mph, but by 40 it’s roughly one in 10 [who survive], and at 50 or 60 it’s essentially over — if you live at all — you’re likely to be permanently, severely injured,” group founder and president Mike Doyle said in a Dec. 30 interview. “Speed kills; speed maims.”

Doyle and volunteer data manager Phil Abendhower are seeking more state support as newly elected Democratic majorities prepare to take control in Richmond. The General Assembly session begins Jan. 14, 2026, and Gov. Abigail Spanberger is scheduled to be inaugurated three days later.

Bill would expand speed-camera enforcement

A central part of the group’s legislative campaign is HB 2041, the Speed Safety Camera Expansion Act, introduced by Del. Holly Seibold, a Fairfax Democrat.

The bill would amend Virginia law to let local governments install speed cameras on state-managed roads designated as part of a high-crash network. Current rules generally restrict the cameras to school and work zones.

Under the proposal, cameras could also be used along high-risk speed corridors and dangerous intersection segments with substantial pedestrian activity, provided the speed limit is no higher than 45 mph. Doyle said potential targets include Route 29, Route 50, Blake Lane, Columbia Pike and Duke Street.

Fine revenue would first pay the cost of operating the camera system. Remaining money would support traffic-safety work. Amendments introduced by Seibold would allow localities to place excess revenue in dedicated funds for planning, designing and building traffic-safety, speed-management and bicycle and pedestrian projects.

Seibold introduced the measure in memory of three Fairfax teenagers who were struck on a Blake Lane sidewalk while walking home from Oakton High School in 2022. The student driver was traveling 81 mph in a 35 mph zone. Leeyan Hanjia Yan, 15, and Ada Gabriela Martinez Nolasco, 14, died from their injuries.

“Most pedestrian fatalities occur outside school zones,” Seibold said in a Jan. 5 email. “Speed safety cameras, as outlined in HB 2041, save lives by reducing speeding on high-crash corridors with persistent safety risks.”

The legislation follows Gov. Glenn Youngkin’s 2025 veto of a similar measure. Doyle called the latest proposal the year’s “single most important, high-impact” traffic-safety bill. He also supports separate legislation involving stop-sign cameras that could collect data about close calls with pedestrians and cyclists.

“On the one hand, there is progress … but we’re far from being satisfied,” Doyle said. He argued that existing technology should be deployed equitably to encourage drivers to slow down and stop.

Five deaths in 22 days

Fairfax County’s 2025 pedestrian death toll began Jan. 29, when Erica Tibbs, 33, was struck at Richmond Highway and Buckman Road.

After scattered crashes during the summer and fall, fatalities surged at the end of the year. Roger Brown, 67, was killed Dec. 7 at Richmond Highway and Backlick Road.

Jimmy Patterson, 50, was found Dec. 23 in a low section of the Fairfax County Parkway median, where he had not been visible to passing traffic for hours.

On Christmas Day, a 75-year-old and a 15-year-old died in an Interstate 495 crash investigated by Virginia State Police.

The final reported pedestrian death of the year occurred Dec. 29 at Route 29 and Hideaway Road. A 53-year-old woman with no fixed address was struck by two vehicles, and police said the second driver fled.

The county recorded about 15 pedestrian fatalities in 2025, down from 20 the previous year. Doyle cautioned that the number of serious injuries remained largely unchanged at approximately 100, leaving many people with life-altering harm that receives less public attention.

“In a perfect world, I would love to see no fatalities at all, and automobiles have come a long way, but you still have these huge vehicles and you have small people,” Abendhower said.

Advocates blame road design

Doyle said traffic deaths cannot be addressed solely by changing individual behavior.

“Safety is a design problem, not a behavioral one,” he said.

He pointed to Route 29 and Fairfax County Parkway, where 12-foot-wide lanes can encourage drivers to travel at highway speeds. Visibility is another concern: four out of five fatal pedestrian crashes in the county happen after dark, frequently in places where lighting is designed around motorists rather than people walking.

Fairfax County police conducted high-visibility enforcement through the Road Shark program in 2025, issuing nearly 50,000 citations. That was a 43% increase from the previous year. Doyle said police enforcement can help but cannot provide continuous coverage everywhere.

Other Northern Virginia lawmakers are pursuing road-engineering changes in the 2026 session. Sen. Danica Roem, a Manassas Democrat, is proposing funding for lighting and pedestrian bridges along Route 28.

“My Route 28 STARS project for Manassas Park and Yorkshire includes innovative intersection designs, a raised median and 1.75 miles of contiguous sidewalk — including pedestrian refuges to make road crossings safer,” Roem said in a Jan. 3 email.

Del. Kathy Tran, a Fairfax Democrat, is seeking stricter engineering standards for the Springfield-Merrifield corridor through HB 42, described in legislative previews as the Safer Roads for Springfield-Merrifield Act.

Her proposal would direct the Virginia Department of Transportation to prioritize safety-focused designs on heavily traveled state roads. Proposed measures include narrowing highway-style 12-foot lanes, adding pedestrian refuge islands to reduce crossing distances on roads such as Old Keene Mill Road and Route 50, and requiring leading pedestrian intervals that give people walking a three- to seven-second head start at intersections.

Northern Virginia Families for Safe Streets also uses reports from residents to identify hazardous intersections before someone is killed. Community members can submit close-call information through the group’s Near Miss Dashboard.

Fairfax County police crash-reconstruction detectives are still searching for the driver of the second unidentified vehicle involved in the Dec. 29 death at Route 29 and Hideaway Road.
