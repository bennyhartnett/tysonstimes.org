McKay Apologizes After Insulting School Board Member in Budget Clash

By Tysons Times Staff

Coverage area
Fairfax
Section
Schools
Published
May 14, 2026
Topics
schools, fairfax, public safety

Article Text

A simmering Fairfax County budget dispute erupted publicly after School Board member Melanie Meren disclosed text messages in which Board of Supervisors Chairman Jeff McKay called her a “bimbo” and accused her of damaging relations between the county’s two governing bodies.

McKay later apologized for the insult, but the exchange drew condemnation from most of Fairfax County’s delegation to the Virginia General Assembly.

The confrontation followed a May 7 newsletter from Meren, who represents the Hunter Mill District. She criticized the Board of Supervisors for eliminating county funding for high school crossing guards and shifting responsibility for middle school programs to Fairfax County Public Schools in the fiscal year 2027 budget adopted May 5.

According to screenshots Meren posted on Facebook, McKay texted her on May 13 and said she should apologize for the “crazy words” in her newsletter.

McKay defended the county’s public safety spending, citing investments in pedestrian safety, school-zone cameras, remaining crossing guards and school resource officers. He also noted that schools received a sizable funding increase while public safety absorbed a cut.

McKay then apparently sent a screenshot of his response to another recipient, describing it as what he had sent “the bimbo.” Meren, who appears to have been included in the group exchange unintentionally, asked whether he meant her.

McKay confirmed that he did, saying Meren had angered county officials and that her newsletter could ultimately cost the school system. He called her criticism exhausting and harmful and said it had set relations between the supervisors and School Board “way back.”

Meren said the two officials do not speak when they are in the same rooms and that her requests to connect had gone unanswered. She offered to discuss their differences by phone or in person.

McKay compared her comments to rhetoric he would expect from Springfield District Supervisor Pat Herrity, the only Republican serving on either board, and said Meren owed County Executive Bryan Hill an apology. Meren believes Hill was the other person included in the text exchange.

McKay also said he would no longer attend Meren’s campaign kickoff on May 17. Meren is seeking a third School Board term ahead of the November 2027 local elections.

Budget cuts ignite the dispute

The argument centers on changes affecting high school crossing guards and the Middle School After School and Values in Prevention programs.

Hill had proposed similar reductions a year earlier while confronting a projected $292.7 million funding gap. Those recommendations included eliminating $4.13 million spent by the Department of Neighborhood and Community Services on the middle school programs and cutting 16 police crossing guard positions assigned to high schools, saving $842,581.

The Board of Supervisors restored that funding in the fiscal year 2026 budget but instructed county and school employees to develop a plan for recovering some future after-school program expenses through fees.

FCPS currently contributes about $1.9 million for supplies, hourly employees and snacks, plus roughly $500,000 for late buses.

County officials had also considered hiring a private contractor for crossing guard duties after staffing shortages caused police to stop covering schools in Herndon and Fort Belvoir. A possible withdrawal from Vienna was discussed but not implemented; town police cover nine locations there.

For fiscal year 2027, which begins July 1, Hill proposed eliminating 13 full-time high school crossing guard positions, saving $1.89 million. Contractors would fill vacancies at elementary and middle schools instead of police patrol officers being reassigned.

His plan, which contained $32.9 million in spending reductions, also transferred the county’s entire financial responsibility for the middle school programs to FCPS.

The VIP summer program already charges registration and field-trip fees. MSAS is free, but Hill’s proposal called for a $300 annual charge for participating students who do not qualify for free or reduced-price meals. Budget documents estimated the fee would raise $1.1 million during the 2026-27 school year.

Supervisors preserved funding for several other services, including the BeWell mental health program, home-delivered meals and a part-time preschool program. They also slightly reduced the real estate tax rate and increased affordable-housing funding.

The adopted budget did not reverse the crossing guard and middle school program changes. It also fell $28.9 million short of the school system’s request, prompting Superintendent Michelle Reid to present potential reductions to the School Board.

Meren argued that transferring responsibility for MSAS effectively allowed supervisors to dictate how FCPS should use its allocation. She also criticized the removal of guards from high schools, where inexperienced teenage drivers and pedestrians converge daily.

She thanked Hunter Mill Supervisor Walter Alcorn for opposing the budget revisions and Franconia District Supervisor Rodney Lusk for attempting to preserve the crossing guard program. Alcorn and Herrity voted against the revisions, with Alcorn objecting to lowering the property tax rate while cutting services used by vulnerable residents.

Meren defends criticism as McKay apologizes

After releasing the messages, Meren said she remained convinced that removing high school crossing guards was wrong for the community. She also accused McKay of acting as though supervisors had unilateral authority over public money.

Meren emphasized that taxpayers fund the government and elected officials must answer for their spending choices. She said attempts to suppress criticism undermine accountability and vowed to continue advocating for student safety.

Several hours after the messages became public, McKay acknowledged that his language was inappropriate.

“I used an inappropriate term while referring to School Board Member Meren that was disrespectful and wrong, and I apologize for it,” he said. McKay added that policy disagreements are legitimate but said he should have expressed himself professionally.

The dispute exposed lingering friction from years of difficult budget negotiations, even as members of both boards have called for better communication and a united campaign for additional state education funding.

The fiscal year 2027 budget transfers $3.05 billion to FCPS, an increase of $118.09 million, or 4.02%, over the previous adopted budget. Of the additional $99 million, however, $4.59 million is tied to middle school programs and transitional services for special education students graduating from high school that FCPS will now be expected to finance fully.

Reid had proposed a $4.1 billion school budget requiring another $138.4 million from the county to cover employee raises and other essential expenses. That request was already substantially smaller than the $268 million increase FCPS sought for fiscal year 2026.

When supervisors did not provide the entire increase last year, school officials made late reductions that included cutting pay raises promised under employees’ first collective bargaining agreement.

State legislators condemn McKay’s language

On May 14, a majority of Fairfax County’s General Assembly delegation issued a joint statement condemning McKay’s remarks and calling on him to treat women with respect.

The legislators described the conduct as outrageous and disturbing, saying women should not face sexist insults for carrying out their public duties. They also argued that using the term in an exchange involving a county employee disrespected both Meren and her elected office.

Their statement defended Meren’s right to question budget decisions affecting her constituents. The lawmakers accused McKay of responding to dissent with name-calling instead of defending the budget on its merits and urged him to uphold Fairfax County’s One Fairfax principles of fairness, justice and equity.

The statement was signed by state Sens. Scott Surovell, Stella Pekarsky, Saddam Salim, Jennifer Boysko and Elizabeth Bennett-Parker, as well as delegates Charniele Herring, Karen Keys-Gamarra, Irene Shin, Karrie Delaney, Dan Helmer, Holly Seibold, Marcus Simon, Vivian Watts, Laura Jane Cohen, Kathy Tran and Rozia Henson.
