Eight Howard Students Took On Virginia’s Jim Crow Bus Law

Article Text

Eight Black women from Howard University boarded a segregated bus in Fairfax County in 1944 and sat near the front, launching a legal confrontation that helped weaken Virginia’s Jim Crow transportation system.

Their largely overlooked protest is now the focus of a nine-minute documentary, “The Student Bus Protest That Challenged Jim Crow,” released by NOVA Parks and Howard University.

The film draws on research and narration by NOVA Parks historian Paul McCray and Howard University historian Sonja Woods. It also includes an interview with Styllene Curtis Boyd, whose mother, Ruby O’Hara Boyd, participated in the protest.

The students had attended a picnic hosted by Caroline Ware, a Howard professor of constitutional history and social sciences, at her Vienna farm. The property was donated to NOVA Parks decades later and became Meadowlark Botanical Gardens.

As the students boarded a bus bound for Washington, D.C., upperclassman Angela Jones initially walked to the back. Boyd, who boarded next, sat directly behind the driver. After seeing the other women take seats near the front, Jones returned and joined them.

The driver ordered the students to move to the rear. One of the women responded that they could sit anywhere because the bus traveled interstate. When they refused to move, the driver left to call police.

The group decided that its four upperclassmen would remain in front and risk arrest. Officers took Marianne Musgrave, Ruth Powell, McLemore and Jones to the Fairfax County jail.

Ware raised $400 for their release, collected them from jail and used her farm as security for their bail. Leon Ransom and Charles Hamilton Houston, Howard faculty members and NAACP lawyers, represented the women in the legal battle that followed.

The Fairfax County Circuit Court convicted the students, who hoped to challenge the ruling before the U.S. Supreme Court. Before the case could reach a state appellate hearing, however, the Fairfax commonwealth’s attorney dropped the charges.

The documentary suggests that prosecutors likely feared the dispute would reach the Supreme Court and result in the segregation law being struck down.

A similar case arrived at the high court a year later. It led to the invalidation of Virginia’s Jim Crow transportation law as applied to buses entering or leaving the state.

Benjamin Talton, executive director of Howard’s Moorland-Spingarn Research Center, said the students’ willingness to refuse their seats, accept arrest and force a court fight reflected a long tradition of Howard women pursuing justice and social change.

McCray describes the protest as one of the breaks that began forming in the system of racial oppression and segregation. Eleven years later, Rosa Parks’ arrest would ignite the Montgomery bus boycott and become a defining event in the national civil rights movement.

The Fairfax County protest resurfaced after McCray discovered references to it in Ware’s correspondence and began examining court files, arrest records and other historical documents.

A sign commemorating the students and their challenge to bus segregation was installed at Meadowlark Botanical Gardens in 2025.
