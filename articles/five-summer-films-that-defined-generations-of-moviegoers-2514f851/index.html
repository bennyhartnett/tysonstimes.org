Five Summer Films That Defined Generations of Moviegoers

Article Text

Looking for classic summer cinema? These five films—spanning the 1970s through the 2010s—deliver everything from coming-of-age adventures to romantic comedies to the movie that invented the summer blockbuster.

Ferris Bueller’s Day Off (1986)

Matthew Broderick’s iconic truant high schooler isn’t quite a traditional protagonist. Ferris Bueller operates more as a godlike figure, delivering sardonic narration while pushing those around him—neurotic best friend Cameron Fry (Alan Ruck) and vengeance-driven Principal Rooney (Jeffrey Jones)—toward transformation. John Hughes packed the film with everything spring freedom demands: an over-the-top parade float sequence, a sprint through chlorine-soaked backyards, Cubs games, and museum outings that double as a love letter to Chicago. The soundtrack remains infectious, particularly Yello’s “Oh Yeah.” As Ferris puts it: “The question isn’t ‘what are we going to do?’ The question is ‘what aren’t we going to do?’”

Stand By Me (1986)

Rob Reiner adapted Stephen King’s novella “The Body” into this Labor Day weekend tale of four 12-year-olds—Gordie Lachance (Wil Wheaton), Vern Tessio (Jerry O’Connell), Chris Chambers (River Phoenix), and Teddy Duchamp (Corey Feldman)—who venture beyond their small town to find the body of a teenager killed in a train accident. They dodge oncoming trains, jump junkyard fences, and outwit an older gang. But the real coming-of-age happens in quiet stretches as they discuss home lives, dreams, and insecurities. Set in 1959 but unmistakably 1980s in spirit, the film refuses rose-colored nostalgia. One friend grows distant. Another dies. Adult Gordie types that gut-wrenching final line: “I never had any friends later on like the ones I had when I was twelve. Jesus, does anyone?”

Jaws (1975)

Steven Spielberg’s shark thriller invented the summer blockbuster—though you might want to avoid thinking about it while swimming. Roy Scheider plays Police Chief Martin Brody, guardian of the peaceful New England tourist town of Amity Island, where a young woman’s fatal shark attack triggers a series of gruesome deaths. With oceanographer Matt Hooper (Richard Dreyfuss) and grizzled ship captain Quint (Robert Shaw), Brody heads to sea for a final confrontation. The film remains a masterclass in suspense. John Williams’ two-note score still unsettles audiences, and the practical effects hold up remarkably well five decades later.

Something’s Gotta Give (2003)

Jack Nicholson plays Harry Sanborn, a playboy with a history of dating younger women, opposite Diane Keaton as Erica Barry, a high-strung playwright. When Harry’s girlfriend brings him to her mother Erica’s Hamptons home for the weekend, mutual irritation gives way to unexpected romance after Harry suffers a heart attack and must extend his stay. Nancy Meyers delivers her signature lived-in, cozy set design alongside stunning coastal imagery. The supporting cast—Keanu Reeves, Frances McDormand, Amanda Peet, Jon Favreau—adds depth to this refreshing take on finding love later in life.

The ‘Before’ Trilogy (1995, 2004, 2013)

Richard Linklater traced a twenty-year love affair across three films. “Before Sunrise” captures the serendipitous day American Jesse (Ethan Hawke) and French Céline (Julie Delpy) spend wandering Vienna, their conversation subtle yet deeply romantic. “Before Sunset” reunites them in Paris nine years later under complicated circumstances—Jesse has a son and a strained marriage; Céline is focused on career and a long-term relationship. “Before Midnight” jumps another nine years to Greece, where long-simmering resentments surface despite the fact they did end up together. The trilogy examines first love’s idealism against the wearying work of making it last—summer in every shade, each installment a new location and chapter in their flawed but beautiful relationship.
