Bad Feedback Can Damage Trust—and Still Fail to Improve Results

Article Text

Feedback is woven into daily life, but much of it misses the mark. When delivered poorly, it can harm relationships, drain motivation and stir resentment without producing the change the giver wanted.

The stakes extend well beyond annual reviews or a supervisor’s hurried correction at the end of a busy week. Feedback includes a driver honking when another motorist hesitates at a green light, a fast walker glaring at someone moving slowly through Tysons Corner or a frustrated sigh over how a partner or child loaded the dishwasher.

It also appears in difficult conversations between friends, attempts to set work-life boundaries, coaching during hobbies and medical advice about lifestyle choices.

Learning to give and receive feedback matters because it touches three basic psychological needs: feeling competent, feeling connected to others and retaining a sense of agency.

Not all feedback serves the same purpose

Feedback generally falls into two categories: evaluation and ongoing guidance.

Evaluation provides a grade, score or ranking. It may compare someone with other people, an established benchmark, their potential or their own standards. Annual performance reviews and school grades are common examples.

Ongoing feedback occurs between formal evaluations. It can reinforce effective behavior or redirect behavior that needs to change. Ideally, this everyday guidance helps someone improve before the next evaluation arrives.

Problems begin when every form of guidance is treated as though it were interchangeable. A performance rating, a compliment and a correction have different purposes, so they should not be delivered in the same way.

Vagueness creates another obstacle. A message that seems obvious to the giver may be confusing to the recipient. People often focus so heavily on their own discomfort that they fail to pause and confirm whether the other person understood.

Simply delivering a message does not make it effective. Feedback succeeds only when it helps improve habits or behavior. If it produces no useful change—or instead causes shame, fear or resentment—it may become destructive.

Match the approach to the message

Evaluative feedback should be honest, specific, clear and considerate. It should also include a practical next step. Telling someone where they fell short without explaining what to do differently leaves them with a judgment but no direction.

Redirecting feedback is generally best offered privately and at a time when the recipient is mentally, physically and emotionally prepared to listen. Privacy protects dignity and reduces the risk of shame, which can undermine trust and motivation while triggering a threat response.

A correction can strike directly at a person’s sense of competence. Harvard Business School researcher Leslie John has described how feedback suggesting someone failed to meet a standard can activate that biological sense of threat. A calm, respectful approach can make it easier for the recipient to remain open to the message.

The giver should also ask for the other person’s perspective. Any observer may possess only part of the story and could be missing important context. Recipients should not be pressed for an immediate response; they may need time to process the conversation before returning to it.

Positive reinforcement works differently. Public recognition can strengthen a person’s sense of competence while showing others which behaviors are effective. People are more likely to repeat actions that receive clear, meaningful praise.

Before giving any kind of feedback, emotions should be under control. Anger and agitation can spread quickly, distort how people interpret a situation and make both sides more likely to misunderstand each other.

Aim for more reinforcement than correction

Healthy relationships also depend on the balance between positive and negative interactions. Research and practice associated with the Gottmans in marriage counseling, as well as leadership development in workplaces, have popularized a five-to-one ratio: at least five positive interactions for every negative one.

Applied to feedback, that means aiming for five moments of reinforcement for each redirection. The praise does not need to be lengthy, but it should be precise.

“I appreciated how you put the mugs on the dishwasher’s top shelf” offers more useful information than a general thank-you. Likewise, telling an employee that they handled an angry customer with patience and grace is more meaningful than saying only that they did a good job.

Receiving imperfect feedback

Although much of the responsibility rests with the giver, recipients still need ways to manage unclear or clumsily delivered feedback.

When requesting input, start by specifying what kind of response would help. Ask for an evaluation, encouragement or coaching instead of making a broad request that invites miscommunication.

Whether the feedback was requested or unexpected, emotional regulation is essential. Deep breathing can help in the moment. It is also useful to identify which kinds of criticism trigger especially strong reactions and consider why. A coach or therapist may help someone explore those patterns.

Recipients do not have to agree immediately—or at all. One measured response is: “Thank you for the feedback. I will reflect on it.” They can also suggest scheduling another conversation after they have had time to think.

People can be proactive by explaining how they prefer to receive feedback. Some dislike public praise. Others need advance notice and benefit from setting a specific time for the discussion.

Finally, even badly presented criticism may contain a small piece of useful information. Looking for the portion that is true, helpful or revealing—even if it represents only 1% of the message—can clarify what the recipient contributed to the situation and what could improve next time.

Receiving feedback does not eliminate personal control. Sometimes that control is limited to choosing how to interpret and respond to the message, but people may have more influence over the process than they initially realize.
