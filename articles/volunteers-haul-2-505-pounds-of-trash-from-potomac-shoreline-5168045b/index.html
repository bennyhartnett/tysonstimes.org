Volunteers Haul 2,505 Pounds of Trash From Potomac Shoreline

A two-hour cleanup along the Potomac River produced a staggering haul: 2,505 pounds of discarded bottles, diapers, tires, furniture and other debris.

By Tysons Times Staff

Fairfax

Published

Article Text

A two-hour cleanup along the Potomac River produced a staggering haul: 2,505 pounds of discarded bottles, diapers, tires, furniture and other debris.

On March 22, 181 volunteers from across Northern Virginia searched the shoreline, the edges of the George Washington Memorial Parkway and the Dyke Marsh Wildlife Preserve. Participants ranged in age from 8 to 80 and contributed more than 400 hours of work, according to Parkway Volunteer Coordinator Scott Hill.

The National Park Service, Friends of Dyke Marsh, Friends of the Mount Vernon Trail, Potomac Conservancy and Blue Star Families Connect sponsored the cleanup.

Volunteers encountered bagged dog waste, cigarette butts, fast-food packaging, soggy socks, combs, bottle caps, lighters and soiled diapers. Larger finds included a mattress, shopping cart, metal grill, lumber, foam pillow, rusted iron pipe and two tires. They also removed a case of empty beer bottles and a distinctive blue vodka bottle.

“Our families were truly honored to take part in the mega-trash event,” said Michelle Black, an outdoor leader for Blue Star Connect Outdoors. “This effort reminded us how important it is to give back, take care of our parks and come together as a community.”

The program connects military families with nearby parks to support well-being and build community ties.

Plastic Waste Remains a Persistent Problem

Cigarette butts and single-use plastics—including bottles and food-service products—are among the items most frequently recovered during local cleanups, Clean Fairfax Director Jen Cole said.

Most cigarette filters contain cellulose acetate, a type of plastic. Butts discarded near buildings and in parking lots can wash into stormwater systems before reaching streams and rivers.

Plastic bottles and caps rank third and fourth among Virginia’s 10 most common types of debris, behind cigarette butts and food wrappers. Worldwide, an estimated 57 million tons of plastic pollution is created annually—enough to fill New York City’s Central Park with waste reaching the height of the Empire State Building.

Litter Free Virginia estimates that roughly 300 million plastic bags reach the Atlantic Ocean. Fairfax County imposed a 5-cent tax on single-use plastic bags in 2022, a measure widely credited with reducing their use and environmental presence.

Polystyrene cups, takeout containers, coolers and packaging pose another challenge because the lightweight material breaks into small pieces that scatter easily and float. Birds and other animals may mistake those fragments for food, sometimes with fatal consequences.

Virginia’s scheduled phaseout requires food vendors belonging to chains with at least 20 locations to stop using polystyrene containers beginning July 1, 2025. Smaller vendors face a July 1, 2026, deadline. After state lawmakers retained those dates, Gov. Glenn Youngkin proposed a March 24 budget amendment that would postpone them until 2028 for large chains and 2030 for smaller businesses.

Tiny Fragments, Lasting Harm

Some plastics can take centuries to decompose, and certain products contain toxic chemicals. Much of the waste instead fragments into microplastics that enter the food web and can be consumed by aquatic organisms, fish, birds and other wildlife. Researchers have also detected microplastics in human bloodstreams and organs.

Bottles and aluminum cans are littered in Virginia at approximately 2.5 times the rate reported in states with container-deposit programs. Those laws require customers to pay a refundable deposit that they recover by returning containers for recycling or reuse.

The national beverage-container recycling rate is about 33%, compared with an average of 70% in states with deposit laws. Despite repeated advocacy in Richmond, the Virginia General Assembly has not approved such a program.

The United States generates 12% of the world’s solid waste despite accounting for less than 5% of its population. Each American produces more than 1,700 pounds of waste annually.

The National Park Service urges visitors to “leave no trace” and pack out whatever they bring. The ton-and-a-quarter removed from this small stretch of Northern Virginia showed how much litter remains behind.
