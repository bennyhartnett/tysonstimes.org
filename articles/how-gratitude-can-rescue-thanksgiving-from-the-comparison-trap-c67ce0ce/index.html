How Gratitude Can Rescue Thanksgiving From the Comparison Trap

Thanksgiving memories can grow sweeter with time, even when they did not feel perfect in the moment.

By Tysons Times Staff

Coverage area
Northern Virginia
Section
Local
Published
November 25, 2025
Topics
local, transportation

Article Text

Thanksgiving memories can grow sweeter with time, even when they did not feel perfect in the moment.

Growing up, my family made the roughly four-hour drive from Austin to Houston each year. Depending on the era, we squeezed into a gray 1984 Buick, a light-blue Dodge Caravan or a teal-toned Suburban. At my aunt’s home, relatives seemed to fill every corner as holiday sights, sounds and aromas blended into one enduring memory.

During my insecure tween and teenage years, however, I measured those gatherings against the polished “traditional” Thanksgivings portrayed in books and advertisements. Friends’ celebrations often sounded grander or somehow more authentic. Believing my family’s version fell short made it harder to appreciate what was happening around me.

That experience reflects a broader connection between comparison and gratitude.

Why comparison hurts less when gratitude grows

People naturally compare themselves with others, with their former selves and with the people they hope to become. Such comparisons can provide motivation, but they can also produce sadness, anger, resentment or frustration.

Gratitude can change that response. It does not prevent people from noticing that someone else appears to have more. Instead, it can make another person’s success feel inspiring rather than threatening while directing attention toward the good already present in one’s own life.

Grateful people also tend to feel more supported by friends and family, which can soften the emotional impact of comparison. Seeing someone who appears less fortunate may deepen appreciation as well, provided that reaction comes from empathy rather than superiority.

By shifting attention from what is missing to what is available, gratitude can support optimism, self-esteem and healthier ways of coping with stress. Differences remain visible, but they become less likely to dominate the way a person evaluates life.

Building gratitude beyond the holiday

Over time, I recognized how fortunate I was to have a crowded house full of loved ones waiting each Thanksgiving. New generations have joined the gathering as older relatives have begun to pass away, making the family’s own tradition more precious with every year.

Gratitude can be strengthened through regular practice, not just on Thanksgiving. Adults and children can record things they appreciate, share them with family or create a “gratitude tree” covered with written reminders of what matters to them.

The goal is not to outrank everyone else on countless measures of success. Any position ahead of or behind another person is temporary. A more lasting aim is to keep growing while appreciating the people, experiences and opportunities already within reach.
